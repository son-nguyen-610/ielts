# Task 2 · Sample 13: Fewer students studying science

*Fewer students are choosing to study science. What are the reasons for this, and what effects does it have on society?*

Dạng 2 phần, vì sao + hệ quả. Thân 1 nguyên nhân · Thân 2 hệ quả chính · Thân 3 Flaw, hệ quả tự giới hạn. Kết không dùng outweigh.

**Model answer**

*Enrolment in science subjects has fallen steadily in many education systems. The principal reason lies in the effort these subjects demand relative to the reward they promise. The main effect falls on industries that depend on technically trained workers, though the shortage eventually corrects part of itself.*

*One primary reason is the mismatch between the workload a science degree demands and the return it offers. Science courses require sustained laboratory work and mathematics that other subjects do not. A student choosing between two degrees compares the hours each will cost against the salary each is likely to produce, and many conclude that the harder path pays no better. A graduate who spends four years in a laboratory and then earns what an accountant earns after three has little reason to recommend the choice to anyone younger.*

*The clearest effect appears in the industries that rely on this training. Engineering, medicine and research all draw from the same pool of graduates. When that pool shrinks, employers compete for a smaller number of candidates and the cost of hiring rises across every sector at once. A hospital and a manufacturer bidding for the same chemistry graduate both pay more and still leave one post unfilled.*

*Nonetheless, the shortage carries a limit of its own. Scarce skills become expensive skills. Once salaries in technical work rise far enough above the alternatives, the calculation that drove students away begins to reverse. A school leaver who sees laboratory graduates earning visibly more than their peers will weigh the extra effort differently.*

*In conclusion, the decline in science enrolment follows directly from the weak return on a demanding degree, and the rising price of technical skill is what limits it. Education systems can shorten that correction by making the reward visible earlier, rather than by urging students toward subjects whose value they cannot yet see.*
