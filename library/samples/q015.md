# Task 2 · Sample 8: Celebrity privacy and the media

*In some countries, celebrities complain about the way the media publicize their private lives. Some people say that they should accept it as part of their fame. To what extent do you agree or disagree?*

Dạng To what extent. A = quyền riêng tư của người nổi tiếng · B = báo chí có quyền đưa tin · Body 3 = Flaw (trách nhiệm giải trình chỉ cần hành vi liên quan đến vai trò công chúng, không cần theo dõi đời tư).

**Model answer — 317 words**

*Proponents of press freedom have long argued that public attention is inseparable from a career built on public visibility. However, critics contend that such coverage leaves famous individuals with no private life whatsoever. From my perspective, celebrities should not be required to accept intrusion into their private lives, largely because fame is granted to their work rather than to their households.*

*One primary reason is that public consent extends only as far as public activity. Actors and musicians agree to be photographed on stage, on screen and at the events their profession requires. Beyond those settings, photographers pursue them into homes, schools and hospitals, where no professional activity takes place and no consent has been given. For example, several performers have relocated or withdrawn from public life entirely after sustained press attention on their children.*

*Nonetheless, the case for media scrutiny has undeniable merits of its own. Public figures endorse products, promote causes and present themselves as models of particular behaviour. Without journalists willing to examine private conduct, a performer who contradicts that image in private faces no consequence for misleading the audience that sustains them. For example, investigations into sponsored athletes have repeatedly exposed conduct that their public campaigns concealed.*

*Even so, accountability of that kind requires access only to conduct bearing on the public role. An athlete who promotes a product while privately contradicting it is a legitimate subject, whereas a photograph taken through a window serves no such purpose. That distinction preserves the scrutiny described above while removing the surveillance, because what the second practice supplies is curiosity rather than information.*

*In conclusion, the right of public figures to a private life outweighs the public's interest in observing it, because genuine accountability requires only conduct affecting their professional role. Ideally, publications should restrict coverage to that conduct and to information the individual has chosen to make public.*
