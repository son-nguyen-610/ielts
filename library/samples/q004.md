# #4 · Task 2: Living alone

*In many countries, a growing number of people are choosing to live alone rather than with family or housemates. Some regard this as a sign of greater personal freedom and independence, while others see it as a worrying social trend. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.*

A = personal freedom · B = the social concern · Body 3 = Feed (living alone is only possible because of the social infrastructure the other side fears is eroding).

**Model answer — 331 words**

*It is often claimed that the rise in single-person households reflects greater personal independence. Others, however, regard it as evidence of weakening social ties. From my perspective, living alone is primarily a sign of independence, largely because it is made possible by the same social development that its critics believe is being lost.*

*One primary reason is that living alone is now a choice rather than a necessity. In previous generations, most adults moved directly from a family home into a shared marital household, because few could afford any alternative. Rising incomes and the expansion of urban housing have removed that constraint, which allows people to decide for themselves when and with whom they live. For example, single-person households become common precisely where incomes rise and cities expand, not where either is absent.*

*Nonetheless, the concerns of critics are not unfounded. Adults who live alone have fewer daily interactions, and the absence of routine contact is associated with loneliness, particularly among older residents. Without another member of the household to notice illness or distress, such problems remain undetected for far longer. For example, local authorities in ageing districts increasingly run welfare visits, precisely because an illness in a single-person household can go unnoticed for weeks.*

*That said, this isolation reflects the design of cities rather than the decision to live alone. Living alone is only viable where public services, transport and dense neighbourhoods already provide the contact that a household once supplied. Where those networks exist, residents who live alone report levels of social contact comparable to those who do not, which means the problem described above arises when infrastructure is absent rather than when households are small.*

*In conclusion, the benefits of greater personal independence outweigh the risks of isolation, because those risks stem from inadequate community infrastructure rather than from the household itself. Ideally, planning authorities should design neighbourhoods that supply regular contact, so that living alone does not become living in isolation.*
